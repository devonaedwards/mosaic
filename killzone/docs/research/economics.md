# Drone production and war economics, as of September 2026

Research for KILL ZONE. This is a **video game** document: the point is to make
the game's build costs, build times and attrition feel recognisable to someone
who follows the subject, not to describe how to do anything.

**Date of research:** 14 September 2026.

## How to read this, and a warning about the sourcing

Every figure below carries two labels.

- **[REPORTED]** — a specific number attributed to a named institution,
  ministry, company or publication.
- **[CLAIM]** — a number asserted by a belligerent (Ukrainian or Russian
  government, intelligence service, or a manufacturer selling the thing). These
  are not lies by default, but nobody audits them and both sides have obvious
  reasons to shade production figures up and loss figures down.
- **[INFERENCE]** — arithmetic or reasoning I did on top of the above. Mine, not
  anyone else's.

Confidence markers are **High** (several independent outlets, consistent over
months), **Medium** (one credible outlet, or several agreeing outlets all
plausibly tracing to one source), **Low** (single source, aggregator site, or an
obviously interested party).

**Sourcing limitation, stated up front.** The network in this environment blocked
direct page fetches, so the figures below come from search-engine result
summaries of the listed articles rather than from reading the articles
end-to-end. The URLs are real and the attributions are as returned. Before any
of these numbers becomes load-bearing for balance, someone should open the
primary links in the source list and confirm the exact figure and date. Treat
every **Medium** and **Low** entry as "probably roughly this, verify before
betting on it." I have invented nothing; where I could not confirm something, it
says so.

## Summary: the twelve things that matter

1. **Both sides are now producing small attack drones in the millions per year,
   and the two sides are within a factor of about 1.5 of each other.** Ukraine
   targets roughly 4.5–7 million FPV-class drones in 2026; Russia claims
   ~7.3 million. **High** that both are in the millions; **Medium** on the exact
   split.
2. **Airframe production is no longer the constraint for either side.** Ukraine's
   installed FPV capacity is credibly reported at 8–10 million/year against
   planned purchases around half that. Idle lines, not full ones, are the story.
   **High.**
3. **The binding constraint on the Ukrainian side is money, then people —
   specifically trained crews and engineers.** Zelensky's own framing in 2026 is
   that Ukraine builds up to 1,000 interceptors a day and cannot crew them.
   **Medium-High.**
4. **The binding constraint on the Russian side looks like components and
   specialist explosives-and-engines, not airframes or money.** **Medium.**
5. **Unit costs fell or held flat through 2025–26 for everything electric and
   rose for anything needing imported specialist parts** (fiber cable, jet
   engines, seekers). **Medium.**
6. **Interception is holding — at around 90% of drones in mass raids — but the
   interceptors were designed against a 185 km/h target and Russia has moved to
   ~500 km/h jet airframes.** This is the single most important trend line in the
   whole subject. **Medium-High.**
7. **Decoys are the cheapest way to buy magazine depth, and Russia plans roughly
   as many decoys as strike drones for 2026** (~50,000 vs ~60,000). **Medium.**
8. **The decisive cost-exchange win of 2025–26 was replacing million-dollar
   missiles with thousand-dollar interceptor drones.** That reversed a 100:1+
   adverse ratio into something like 3:1 to 20:1 favourable. **High** on the
   direction, **Medium** on the numbers.
9. **Fiber-optic drones are the expensive, jam-proof premium tier, and the
   consumable (the cable) is the chokepoint, not the airframe.** **Medium-High.**
10. **China is the chokepoint for both sides, and asymmetrically so — the
    controls bite Ukraine harder than Russia.** **High** on the reliance,
    **Medium** on the asymmetry.
11. **Ground robots went from nothing to a real logistics arm in eighteen months**
    — tens of thousands of missions a month by mid-2026. **Medium-High.**
12. **Western production is irrelevant at these volumes.** The Pentagon's flagship
    programme will have ordered under 200,000 drones cumulatively by early 2027,
    against Ukraine's ~500,000 a month. **High.**

**The Shahed cost spread is the single worst-behaved number in this document.**
Ukrainian sources quote $10,000 when they want to make interception look
expensive relative to the threat; CSIS uses ~$35,000; other analysis uses a
$20,000–50,000 band. These are probably measuring different things — marginal
build cost at Alabuga, versus fully-loaded cost including the seeker and the
imported parts, versus a jet variant. **Do not treat any single Shahed price as
authoritative.** For a game, pick a number in the middle and move on.

### How costs moved, 2025 → 2026

- **Down or flat: anything all-electric and locally sourced.** Interceptors fell
  from roughly $3,500 toward $1,000–2,500 as volume arrived. A long-range AI FPV
  was cut to ~$500 while its range increased. The Vampire came in under $10,000.
  Mass production did what mass production does. **Medium confidence.**
- **Up: anything requiring an imported specialist input.** Chinese fiber-optic
  material prices to Russian buyers rose 2.5–4× from 2026. Ukrainian long-range
  drone production was reported as slowed by a **mini jet engine shortage**
  [REPORTED, Low — single outlet]. Explosive fill got globally tighter.
  **Medium confidence.**
- **Up in aggregate for the offence, per effective round.** Russia shifting from
  piston Geran-2 to jet Geran-4/5 buys speed at the cost of a more expensive
  airframe with a harder-to-source engine. [INFERENCE, Medium.]

The pattern is clean and it is the pattern a game should model: **commodity
electronics deflate under volume; specialist inputs inflate under demand.**

## Cost-exchange ratios

| Exchange | Ratio | Basis | Label / confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Patriot PAC-3 MSE vs Shahed | **106:1 to 149:1 against the defender** | $3.7–5.2m missile vs ~$35,000 drone | [REPORTED] Medium |
| Interceptor drone vs Shahed | **roughly 3:1 to 20:1 in the defender's favour** | $1,000–3,500 interceptor vs $10,000–35,000 Shahed | [INFERENCE] Medium |
| Interceptor drone vs Shahed, allowing for misses | **~2:1 to 12:1 favourable** | as above, ÷ claimed 80–90% single-shot hit rate, so ~1.1–1.25 interceptors per kill | [INFERENCE] Medium |
| Interceptor vs **decoy** | **roughly 3:1 to 10:1 in the defender's favour on paper** | $1,000–3,500 vs $10,000 Gerbera | [INFERENCE] Medium |
| Interceptor vs decoy, real terms | **plausibly a defender loss** | the decoy also consumes a radar track, a crew's attention and a sortie, none of which is priced | [INFERENCE] Low — this is reasoning, not a finding |
| FPV vs main battle tank | **order 100:1 to 1,000:1 in the attacker's favour** | ~$400 FPV, 1–6 per kill, vs a tank costing millions | [INFERENCE] Low — I found no 2026 source stating drones-per-tank-kill |
| Rubicon FPV expenditure per claimed target | **~3.6 drones per target struck** | 1m drones / 280,000 targets, Jan 2025–Aug 2026 | [INFERENCE] Medium, on a [CLAIM] |
| Yolka vs large Ukrainian drone | **3+ interceptors per kill** | stated Russian requirement for large targets | [CLAIM] Low |

Two things to take from this table.

**First, the defensive economics were rescued by the interceptor drone, and only
by the interceptor drone.** Every SAM-based exchange is catastrophic for the
defender and always will be. Defense News put it plainly in March 2026: novel
interceptor drones "bend air-defense economics in Ukraine's favor". That is the
hinge of the entire 2025–26 period.

**Second, the decoy is the counter-move to the interceptor, and it works by
attacking the thing the interceptor cannot make cheaper: the crew's time.** A
$10,000 decoy costing the defender a $2,000 interceptor looks like a defender
win in dollars and is a defender loss in the currency that is actually scarce.
I want to be explicit that this is **[INFERENCE]** — I did not find a source
stating it — but it follows directly from the reported facts that Russia plans
~50,000 decoys for 2026, that decoys are now being fitted with strike seekers,
and that Ukraine's stated shortage is operators rather than airframes.

## Where the binding constraint actually is

### Money — the Ukrainian constraint. Evidence: strong.

- Ukraine's defence sector "can produce $35–40 billion worth of equipment
  annually" but much of that capacity is unused for lack of funding [REPORTED,
  Medium — New Voice of Ukraine, "defense plants sit idle as funding gap chokes
  war effort"].
- Ukraine's 2026 defence budget shortfall is reported at ~UAH 1.1 trillion,
  about **$27 billion** [REPORTED, Medium].
- Installed FPV capacity 8–10 million/year against ~4.5 million planned
  purchases [REPORTED, Low].
- Partner financing of Ukrainian-built weapons (the "Danish model") drew **$6.7
  billion in 2025** [REPORTED, Medium]; the EU signed a **$1.1 billion** drone
  deal [REPORTED, Medium]; the European Commission took preparatory steps on
  further drone-production support in April 2026 [REPORTED, Medium].

The characterisation that keeps recurring in 2026 reporting is that Ukraine
"once lacked factories, now lacks purchase orders". **This is the single most
important economic fact for a game designer.** Ukraine's drone economy is
demand-constrained, not supply-constrained.

### Components — the constraint on both sides. Evidence: strong.

- ~97% of Ukrainian UAS producers name China as a primary import source
  [REPORTED, Medium — FDD, October 2025].
- The Chinese-parts share of Ukrainian drones fell to ~38% by 2025 as
  localisation accelerated [REPORTED, Medium — Kyiv Post]. These two figures are
  not contradictory: almost everyone still buys *something* from China, but the
  bill-of-materials share has fallen.
- China restricted >7 kg drone exports to both belligerents in June 2023, then on
  1 September 2024 extended controls to flight controllers, carbon frames,
  motors, radio modules and navigation cameras [REPORTED, High].
- CSIS argues the restrictions specifically weaken Ukraine's negotiating position
  [REPORTED, Medium]; The Diplomat reported in April 2026 that the same controls
  hit Europe's emerging drone doctrine [REPORTED, Medium]; RFE/RL in August 2026
  described Ukraine's "uncomfortable reliance" on China [REPORTED, Medium].
- Russian decoy drones **depend on Western parts** [REPORTED, Medium — Institute
  for Science and International Security]. Both sides are importing from the
  other's economic bloc.

### Explosives — a real constraint, mostly for the West. Evidence: moderate.

- A global TNT shortage is attributed to the war [REPORTED, Medium — CNBC,
  Militarnyi].
- Europe's RDX and TNT chains are still rebuilding; Lithuania imports nearly all
  explosive fill for its drone and artillery programmes [REPORTED, Medium].
- Poland's Nitro-Chem supplies the US under a ~$310 million contract and agreed
  in March 2026 to a second line doubling its ~10,000 t/yr output over three to
  four years [REPORTED, Medium].

I found **no** evidence that explosive fill is currently capping Ukrainian or
Russian FPV output. An FPV warhead is a few hundred grams to a couple of
kilograms, often a repurposed RPG or mortar round. Explosives bite hardest on
artillery and on Western programmes trying to start from a standing start.

### Airframes — not a constraint. Evidence: strong.

Idle Ukrainian lines, 160+ FPV firms, ~280 UGV firms, 15,000 Russian FPV a day.
Nobody on either side is short of airframes.

### Crews — the constraint that everyone names. Evidence: moderate to strong.

- Zelensky: Ukraine produces up to **1,000 interceptors per day, and there are
  not enough operators** [CLAIM, Medium — liga.net]. This is the clearest
  statement of the whole thesis and it comes from the head of state, which makes
  it both authoritative and politically motivated.
- Training a pilot from scratch to basic operational level takes **at least three
  months** [REPORTED, Medium].
- Units report not just a shortage of pilots but a shortage of "technically
  skilled and motivated recruits" [REPORTED, Medium].
- Ukraine has explored training **civilian Territorial Defence volunteers** to
  fly interceptors over their own cities [REPORTED, Medium] — the classic
  response to a crew ceiling.
- Bloomberg, 25 August 2026: a **shortage of engineers** has become a bottleneck
  for Ukraine's defence-tech sector [REPORTED, Medium-High]. Note this is a
  different people-shortage from the crew shortage, and arguably the deeper one.
- Against this: Militarnyi reported that Ukraine's air defence units have
  **filled** the interceptor shortage [REPORTED, Medium]. The picture is not
  uniform.

**Verdict.** For Ukraine, in order: money → skilled people (engineers and crews)
→ specific imported components → airframes. For Russia, in order: components and
specialist engines → skilled crews → money → airframes. Russia's Rubicon growing
from ~1,450 to ~5,000 personnel, and from 8 detachments of 149 to 12 of 474
[REPORTED, Medium], is a state spending heavily to solve a crew problem it
clearly believes it has.

## The magazine war: is defence keeping up?

**Yes, for now, and the margin is thin.**

The reported interception numbers hold up well. Ukraine's MoD claimed ~90% of
aerial threats downed in large-scale June 2026 attacks [CLAIM, Medium; carried by
GlobalSecurity, UK Defence Journal and defence-industry.eu]. March 2026: 5,833 of
6,463 drones intercepted, **90.25%** [CLAIM, Medium]. Against conventional piston
Shaheds specifically, 92–96% and reportedly as high as 96% in mid-2026 periods
[CLAIM, Medium]. Interceptor drones alone downed **more than 33,000 Russian UAVs
in March 2026** [CLAIM, Medium — Fedorov], and Fedorov claimed the interceptor
kill rate doubled over four months to May 2026 [CLAIM, Medium].

At the same time, Shahed-type launches rose from ~144/day in April to ~167/day in
May 2026, the highest recorded [REPORTED, High — Institute for Science and
International Security], with monthly launch volumes reported as rising ~35%.

So: **a rising interception rate against a rising launch rate, netting out to a
roughly constant number of leakers.** At 167 launches/day and 90% interception,
about **17 drones get through per day** [INFERENCE, High — arithmetic]. At 90%
and 250/day, it would be 25. The defender has to keep improving just to hold
absolute damage constant. This is the magazine war in one sentence.

**The thing that could break it** is stated bluntly in the 2026 reporting: the
interceptors "that closed Ukraine's cost-exchange gap were built against a
185 km/h target and cannot catch a 500 km/h one" [REPORTED, Medium — United24].
Russia's jet Geran-4/5 output has overtaken piston Geran-2 output [CLAIM,
Medium], and Russia intends jets to be 50% of the fleet [CLAIM, Medium].

If interception against jet variants settles materially below 90%, the
consequences are not linear. Leakage roughly triples going from 90% to 70%
interception at constant launch volume [INFERENCE, High]. The defender then has
three moves, all reported as being attempted: build faster interceptors, build
more interceptors and crew them, or fall back onto guns and missiles and accept
the ruinous exchange ratio.

## Supply chains and chokepoints

**China is the chokepoint for the small-drone economy on both sides.** Batteries,
flight controllers, navigation modules, thermal sensors, motors, carbon frames.
Ukraine cut the Chinese share of its bill of materials to ~38% by 2025 but ~97%
of producers still import from China. The documented effects of restriction are:
Ukrainian producers facing supply insecurity and price rises; Russia receiving
comparatively better access and active assistance in circumventing controls;
and, per The Diplomat in April 2026, European drone programmes being hit by the
same controls.

**Fiber-optic cable is the chokepoint for the jam-proof tier,** and it is the
one case where a documented physical attack visibly changed an economy. Russia's
only fiber-optic plant, at Saransk — six lines, ~2,000 km/day each, ~12,000
km/day total — was struck in April–May 2025 and **had still not resumed
operations nearly a year later** [REPORTED, Medium — Militarnyi]. Russia needed
≥6 million km of cable in 2025 [REPORTED, Medium]. With domestic production
down, Chinese material prices to Russian buyers rose 2.5–4× from 2026 [REPORTED,
Medium]. Ukraine is in the same boat from the other direction: it lags on fiber
drone production precisely because it depends on imported cable.

This is the cleanest documented case in the whole subject of **a strike on one
factory moving the price of a whole weapon category.** It is an excellent model
for a strategy game.

**Jet engines** are reported as a constraint on Ukrainian long-range drone
output [REPORTED, Low — single outlet]. Worth flagging, not worth building on
without verification.

**Explosive fill** is a Western problem more than a belligerent one, as above.

## Crew and operator training throughput

The reported figures are thin and I want to be honest about that.

- At least **three months** from zero to basic operational competence [REPORTED,
  Medium].
- By spring 2026 the **majority** of new Ukrainian pilots went through formal
  flight schools rather than learning in their units [REPORTED, Medium].
- **>10,000 operators** trained since the start of one training programme
  [REPORTED, Medium] — note this is one programme, not the national total, and
  the national total is not something I found.
- Freedom Sky had trained **>1,000** people on interceptor types as of 2026
  [REPORTED, Medium].
- Kyiv Post's framing: Ukraine "probably has the world's best drone pilot
  training, also the biggest" [REPORTED, Low — this is commentary].

Set that against **1,000 interceptors a day** of production. Even assuming one
crew flies many sorties and many airframes, a thousand interceptors a day is
tens of thousands of sorties a month, and a training pipeline measured in
thousands of graduates cannot obviously feed it. Zelensky's own statement — we
build them, we cannot crew them — is consistent with the arithmetic.

**Is crew the real ceiling?** My assessment: **for interceptors, yes, and it is
stated as such by the Ukrainian president. For front-line FPV, probably not —
there money and components bite first. For engineering and integration talent,
emphatically yes,** and Bloomberg's August 2026 reporting on the engineer
shortage is the strongest evidence in this document for a people-constraint.
Confidence: **Medium.** I would like more than one presidential statement and one
Bloomberg headline before calling this settled.

## Western and other foreign production: does it matter at these volumes?

**No. Not remotely, as of September 2026.**

The Pentagon's Drone Dominance Program — a $1.1 billion effort — will have
ordered **just under 200,000 drones cumulatively by February 2027** [REPORTED,
High — Reuters/US News, 27 July 2026]. The same Pentagon official, Travis Metz,
states Ukraine will produce **6–7 million small FPV drones in 2026, roughly
500,000 a month.** The entire multi-year American programme is about **two and a
half weeks of Ukrainian output** [INFERENCE, High — arithmetic on the two stated
figures].

The Pentagon has said this itself, repeatedly and publicly, through 2026: US
industry is "still years from matching Ukraine's wartime output", and the gap "is
measured in years" [REPORTED, High].

Europe is in a similar position, with the additional difficulty that Chinese
export controls hit European drone programmes too [REPORTED, Medium]. Europe's
meaningful contribution is **money routed into Ukrainian factories** — the Danish
model's $6.7 billion in 2025, the EU's $1.1 billion drone deal — not units built
in Europe. Ukraine, correspondingly, is moving into the export role: the Vampire
was being offered for export at BEDEX 2026 [REPORTED, Medium], and Ukraine has
positioned itself as a school for foreign drone operators [REPORTED, Medium].

Where foreign production **does** matter is in the specialist tail: Polish
explosive fill, Western components inside Russian decoys, Chinese everything.
Nobody outside the two belligerents is building small attack drones at
war-relevant volume.

## What this implies for the game's economy

### Is "airframes are cheap and crews are the cap" the right model?

**Mostly yes, with one important correction and one addition.**

**The correction: it is not crews first, it is money first.** The best-evidenced
single fact in this document is that Ukraine's drone factories sit partly idle
for want of purchase orders, against an $27 billion budget gap. A game that
models drone production as limited by crews alone will miss the thing that
actually governs 2026: a player who cannot pay cannot build, even with the
factory standing there. If KILL ZONE has a resource economy at all, the honest
model is **money gates production; crews gate employment.** Two separate valves
on the same pipe.

**The addition: decoys attack the crew valve, not the money valve.** This is the
most interesting mechanic available in the whole subject and it is barely
modelled anywhere. A decoy that costs the attacker $10,000 and consumes a
defender's $2,000 interceptor is a bad trade in dollars and a good trade in the
currency that matters, because it also consumes a crew-sortie and a sensor track.
If the game has interceptor crews with a sortie rate, decoys become genuinely
strategic rather than a cosmetic unit.

### Concrete recommendations

1. **Make small airframes near-free and crew slots hard-capped.** The 2026
   evidence supports an extreme version of this: production in the millions,
   trained crews in the thousands. A ratio of **1,000 airframes per trained crew
   per year** is defensible [INFERENCE from Ukrainian FPV output ÷ plausible crew
   numbers; Low confidence on the exact figure, High on the order of magnitude].
2. **Give crews a training time, not a purchase price.** Three months to basic
   competence is the reported figure. In a game compressing time, that should be
   the longest build queue in the roster — longer than any airframe, longer than
   most buildings. Crews should be the thing you regret losing.
3. **Price the tiers roughly as reported.** FPV 1× (~$400). Interceptor 3–8×.
   Heavy multirotor ~25×. Decoy ~25×. Loitering/Shahed-class 25–90×. Deep-strike
   140× (FP-1) to 500× (Liutyi). SAM 10,000×. That last gap is the point: the
   game should make firing a missile at a drone feel like a defeat.
4. **Model the fiber premium as a consumable, not an airframe cost.** The
   airframe is the same; the spool is the expense and the chokepoint. A fiber
   drone that costs the same to build but draws from a separate, strikeable
   stockpile is both accurate and better play.
5. **Make one enemy factory worth striking.** Saransk is the precedent: one plant
   struck, a year of lost production, a 2.5–4× price rise in a whole category. A
   single deep-strike target that raises the opponent's fiber-drone cost for the
   rest of the match is historically grounded.
6. **Let interception rates be high and let the leakage still hurt.** 90%
   interception is the reported reality, and 10% of a large number is still a
   large number. High interception with meaningful leakage is both accurate and
   much better drama than a binary shield.
7. **Build in a speed obsolescence event.** Interceptors tuned to a 185 km/h
   target cannot catch a 500 km/h one. An opponent tech upgrade that invalidates
   a defensive investment is the best-attested dynamic of 2026 and would make an
   excellent mid-game turn.
8. **Ground robots are logistics, not combat.** The reported use is >100,000
   logistics and casualty-evacuation missions, growing ~15% a month. Model them
   as supply throughput and recovery, not as tanks.
9. **Do not give the player a Western drone industry.** At these volumes it
   would be a lie. Foreign powers supply **money, specialist components and
   explosive fill** — good strategic levers, and more interesting than another
   build option.

### What I could not establish

- Any reliable **drones-per-vehicle-kill** figure for 2026. The Rubicon 3.6
  drones-per-target-struck number is the closest, and it is a belligerent claim
  about an elite unit striking all target types.
- **Fiber-optic FPV unit cost** for 2026. I have the cable price movement but not
  a delivered airframe price.
- **Heavy multirotor production counts** for either side.
- **National** drone-crew training throughput for either side, as opposed to
  individual programmes.
- Whether the **jet-engine shortage** for Ukrainian long-range drones is real —
  single low-quality source.
- A source stating the **decoy-versus-interceptor** exchange explicitly. That
  section is my reasoning on top of reported facts, and is marked as such.
